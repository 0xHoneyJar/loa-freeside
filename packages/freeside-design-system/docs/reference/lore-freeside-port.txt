## Babylon port is not an official place-name

“Babylon port” is what the Zionites call **Freeside’s port**, not necessarily a terminal formally named *Babylon Port*. In their Rastafarian vocabulary, **Babylon** means the corrupt terrestrial–corporate civilization represented by Freeside, Tessier-Ashpool, banks, tourism, luxury, policing, and artificial intelligence. The same conversation contrasts Zion’s religious law with “Babylon’s law.”

## What it probably looks like

Gibson never provides a complete exterior establishing shot of the port. The most defensible reconstruction is a **high-capacity orbital docking and customs complex embedded in Freeside’s rotating hull**, followed almost immediately by an extravagant commercial boulevard.

### Outside the spindle

Approaching aboard the *Marcus Garvey*, Case sees Freeside only through a docking display: the tug’s course is represented by red dots heading toward a **segmented green circle**. That circle suggests the immense circular cross-section of the rotating spindle rather than a conventional space station with a single central hub.

Visually, I would depict the exterior port as:

* A monumental curved hull disappearing in both directions.
* Multiple recessed docking apertures distributed across or near the spindle’s structural zones.
* Pressure-lock collars, articulated gangways, navigation lights and automated grappling equipment.
* Shuttles, yachts, tugs, freight containers and maintenance vehicles moving around it.
* Large polished corporate sections beside older, patched industrial hardware used by independent operators like the Zionites.
* Almost no romantic “airport terminal” façade—the impressive object is the **scale of the habitat itself**.

That industrial machinery is partly inferential, but it matches other docking systems Gibson describes. At the Straylight dock, automated arms seize a yacht while an articulated, hydraulically supported gangway reaches around the vessel to a smaller tug.

### The customs threshold

The port’s social character is clearer than its architecture. Freeside customs is remarkably light: entry consists principally of **demonstrating that you possess adequate credit**. Maelcum, the working-class Zionite pilot, remains aboard his tug while Case and Molly enter.

So the customs area should not resemble an intimidating terrestrial border checkpoint. It would more likely feel like:

* A quiet, frictionless financial screening zone.
* Automated identity and credit terminals.
* Discreet security rather than conspicuous armed guards.
* Clean composite walls, illuminated routing strips and sealed pressure doors.
* Concierge-like employees directing solvent visitors onward.
* Separate freight, crew and commercial channels hidden behind the polished passenger route.

The governing principle is not citizenship but **creditworthiness**. Freeside does not ask, “Who are you?” so much as, “Can you pay?”

## What you see immediately after entering

The first visible commercial establishment after Case reaches the inner surface is a branch of the **Beautiful Girl coffee franchise**. This is important: the grand orbital frontier immediately resolves into branded retail.

The port then opens onto **Rue Jules Verne**, which is much more vividly described:

* A very broad street occupying the bottom of a deep architectural canyon.
* Shops and buildings angled so subtly that both ends of the street disappear from view.
* Balconies and overhanging tiers climbing above the street.
* Dense green vegetation spilling downward from those terraces.
* A brilliant linear artificial sun—the Lado-Acheson armature—high overhead.
* A recorded Mediterranean-blue sky resembling Cannes.
* Buildings, casinos, lakes and streets continuing around the impossible interior curvature of the spindle.

Therefore the transition through Babylon port should feel abrupt:

> **patched orbital tug → sealed docking machinery → nearly invisible credit check → coffee franchise → impossible luxury city beneath an artificial Mediterranean sky**

## The strongest visual interpretation

Babylon port should look like the **backstage loading entrance to paradise**.

The docking side is metallic, pressurized and operational: dark vacuum outside, cold hull plating, grappling machinery, freight traffic and compact spacecraft. Passing through customs, the architecture becomes smoother and more expensive. The corridors broaden. Advertisements and commercial signage appear. Then the final doors open onto Rue Jules Verne, where vegetation hangs above boutiques and the city curves upward around the visitor.

It should not look dirty or lawless like Chiba’s port. Nor should it look completely ceremonial. It is a **commercial intake valve** designed to ingest tourists, money, workers, contraband and freight and then dissolve them into Freeside’s entertainment economy. Molly later characterizes Freeside as an enormous tube through which practically everything is poured—tourists, hustlers and anything else the habitat can monetize.

The emotional effect is:

**industrial outside, frictionless at the border, spectacular inside—and quietly exclusionary throughout.**
